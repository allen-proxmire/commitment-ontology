# The web behind the lattice

The lattice is a stand-in. What CO has is a web of relations; the cubic grid is the simplest web to compute on. The picture held is a grid drawn by hand, wiggly, not graph paper and not a crystal; perhaps set more evenly where things commit. Readings so far (run C15, RECORD_OF_METHOD/MATTER_runs/C15_wiggly_grid; two dimensions, massless pattern; r = ½ in c15a–d, plain hops with r = 0 in c15e–k).

## Two kinds of wiggle

*A wiggle in the drawing.* Places displaced and relations longer or shorter, with each hop's strength following the local geometry: its relation's length and direction and the size of the cells around it (length and direction alone are not enough beyond one dimension). To a long-wavelength pattern this should be the even grid in other coordinates, with scattering entering only at a higher order. Run so far (c15f–h, two dimensions, places displaced independently by 0.15 hops):
- With the grid's four neighbours no weights of this form (a size per cell, a vector per link) make patterns follow the true positions: a place has six conditions to meet (a constant pattern has no gradient; a pattern linear in the true positions has its true gradient) and five weights to meet them with. The best weights leave a first-order leftover, 0.08k on a plane wave, and the pattern scatters (power of k 3.6 to 3.9 in the runs).
- With eight neighbours (the diagonals linked too) weights meeting the conditions exactly exist, and the leftover on a plane wave falls to second order, 0.17k².
- The loss itself was not lower with them at k = 0.33 to 0.79. Cause open (candidates: the two leftovers are of one size there; scattering into the naive hops' doubled states, which first-order exactness does not suppress).
- A wiggle alike over several hops, on the bare grid: with the displacement smooth over 2 hops the loss falls about 90-fold against an independent one of the same size (4 × 10⁻⁶ against 3.5 × 10⁻⁴ per hop time at k = 0.33; neighbour-to-neighbour difference 0.05 against 0.21, which alone would give 16-fold); smooth over 4 hops and four times larger, still about 10-fold lower (difference 0.105; 4-fold from that alone). One seed; a floor near 5 × 10⁻⁶ is not resolved (c15i). These wiggles are flat (a redrawing), so the run shows smooth variation is not felt as roughness; bending by a curved one is not tested here.

*No drawing.* With no backdrop a place has no position apart from the web: the web's geometry is its hop strengths, and a drawing adds nothing a pattern can read. Even hops with the grid's neighbours, on any drawing, are graph paper. So the question is only how the strengths vary. Varying smoothly over several hops is gravity's kind: not felt as roughness, and bending patterns only as gravity does. Varying independently from hop to hop is felt, and held to the limits in the table below. A test of flatness that uses no drawn positions is a silence. Whether strengths that vary hop to hop can still be flat, with too few relations per place to tell, is where the count above bites: candidate, not settled.

## Seams

Two grids turned by 45° against each other, meeting along a line. Head-on, with only every other row linked across, about 3% or more of a packet is turned back at long wavelength for every link strength tried (six values, 0.7 to 2.0), rising to 57% at k = 0.8. With every row linked and the two strengths chosen (a coarse scan, its best value at the scan's edge), 0.2% is turned back at long wavelength, rising to 2% at k = 0.8. A seam can be made nearly clean by the strengths of its relations; a crude one cannot. At long wavelength half of a beam is lost after about 23 crude seams or 350 of the better ones. Limits: the turned grid here also has half the places, so the turn and the change of density are not separated. Grade: reading.

## What a rule for relations would have to give

- Each relation's hop strength following its length and direction, to the accuracy above.
- Which relations a newborn place has, and how places change neighbours where they slip or shear: silence.
- Whether the web is set more evenly where things commit: a local rule can do it, slowly, where a place has relations enough (run c15k: with eight neighbours the leftover falls round by round, about as n^−0.4; with four it sticks; the rule there steers toward drawn positions, so it is a stand-in). Commitments look too few to reach the voids: 7 × 10⁻⁶ per place over the universe's age, 4 to 5 orders short, counting nucleons at today's void density with each renewal reaching a nucleon's own size. That flips if a free nucleon's pattern spans its whole wave packet (about 10⁷ per place), and leaves out dark patterns, relic neutrinos and the denser past. If it holds, evenness to the table's limits has to come with a place's birth, since the far-travelled neutrino crossed voids. Candidate.

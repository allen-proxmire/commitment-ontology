# Triangles and gears

A picture of the quarks and the lightest nuclei.

Everything this picture is matched against is known or observed. Physics has the facts and the theory that computes them; it has no agreed picture of what is there underneath. CO tests itself as that connecting story. The picture is posited: a way the facts could make sense in CO's terms. Its rules are chosen, and possibility does not say why it behaves this way. Nothing here is derived, and nothing here is new physics.

## The picture

- A quark is a triangle that carries its kind, up or down.
- A nucleon is three triangles: one point down, set between two that point up. The one in the middle is the odd quark, the two beside it the alike pair. A down between two ups is the proton; an up between two downs is the neutron. The two are one shape with the kinds swapped.
- The group shows a different face each way: one side on top (the odd quark's), two sides underneath (the alike pair's), and a side with a corner to the left and to the right.

## The rules

1. **One fits two.** A single side bonds to a double side of the same kind. Together they make three.
2. **Triangles that touch along a side turn opposite ways.** Each triangle turns one of two ways; the turning is its spin. A touching that breaks this rule is weak: a join that breaks it does not hold alone.
3. **Alike ones are opposite.** Two protons in one bound body turn opposite ways, and two neutrons likewise. This is exclusion, put in. With two ways of turning, one body holds at most two of each.

A nucleon's own turning is its alike pair's.

## What follows, against what is observed

| In the picture | Observed |
|---|---|
| Inside a nucleon the two alike quarks each touch the odd one and not each other. So the alike pair turns together and the odd one against: a net of one turn | The proton and neutron have spin ½, and in the quark model the alike pair is spin-aligned. The fixed arrangement gives the ratio of the two magnetic moments as −5/4; the quark model's full mixture gives −3/2; measured −1.46 |
| Three quarks all turning one way break rule 2 | The Δ, three quarks with spins aligned, is 293 MeV heavier than the nucleon and short-lived |
| Stacked, a proton's two ups fit a neutron's one up, and a neutron's two downs fit a proton's one down. Two protons do not fit, nor two neutrons | The deuteron is the only bound pair |
| The two stackings are the same drawing with the kinds swapped | Nuclear forces are nearly the same with protons and neutrons swapped (helium-3 and tritium are mirror partners), and part of the force exchanges the two |
| At a join the single touches both of the pair, so it turns against them. That makes the two nucleons turn the same way. With the nucleons opposed the three at the join all turn alike: a weak join | The deuteron has spin 1. The pair with spins opposed is not bound, though it nearly is |
| Three: the two alike nucleons are opposite (rule 3), so one join is full and one is weak, and the spin left over is the unpaired nucleon's | Helium-3: spin ½, magnetic moment −2.13 against the neutron's −1.91. Tritium: spin ½, 2.98 against the proton's 2.79 |
| Four in a ring, joins full and weak by turns: every bonding face is used and the spins cancel. Rule 3 allows no larger body | Helium-4: spin 0, bound far more tightly than its neighbours (7.1 MeV a nucleon against 1.1 for the deuteron and 2.6 to 2.8 for the threes) |
| A closed ring has no bonding face left: a fifth cannot bond, and two rings do not bond | No stable nucleus of five; beryllium-8 falls apart into two helium-4 |

## Where it misses

- **Beyond four there is nothing.** By rule 3 a body holds two of each, and a closed ring bonds to nothing. Lithium, beryllium, carbon and the rest need another state for the extra particles and another kind of join. In the standard account the extra particles take the next state, which carries one unit of orbital motion; the picture has no orbital motion.
- **A second join is not settled.** A side set into the notch between two unlike triangles would let a straight pair hang on a ring (lithium-6: spin 1, magnetic moment 0.82 against the deuteron's 0.86) and a neutron sit between two rings (beryllium-9). As it stands it is too easy: it would also bind two protons, a fifth on a ring (helium-5 is unbound) and ring to ring. It gives lithium-7 and beryllium-9 spin ½ where they have 3/2.
- **The three at a join are not a whole.** Three alike quarks, two one way and one the other, is the arrangement quark exclusion forbids for a real three-quark particle.
- **No charge, no parity, no shape.** Tritium and helium-3 differ in binding, heavy nuclei hold more neutrons than protons, and the deuteron is slightly stretched; none of these is in the picture.
- **No masses and no energies** come out, only yes or no and the spins.

## What is put in

Like kinds bond, with no reason given. Exclusion. Two ways of turning. That a join breaking rule 2 is weak and holds only beside a full one. The triangle and the count of three. The standard reasons are different: a force that depends on spin, and exclusion.

## Standing

Posited: a picture, not a derivation. Rules 1 and 2 were chosen to match the proton and the deuteron; exclusion and the weak join were added to reach three and four. With those four choices it gives the nucleon's spin, the one bound pair and its spin, the spins of the threes, helium-4's closure and spin, and nothing beyond four, which matches the gaps at five and eight and misses the rest. It matches known answers and says nothing new. A nucleus of four or fewer whose binding or spin the rules get wrong would count against it.

In CO's terms a bound whole is a now that keeps being shared (reading; [`how_patterns_combine.md`](how_patterns_combine.md)). The picture is one way to draw that.

# A proposed check: can gravity alone entangle two masses?

*For groups building matter-wave and levitated-mass experiments toward gravitationally mediated entanglement. One page; detail in [`OPEN_DIRECTIONS_notes/prediction_map.md`](../OPEN_DIRECTIONS_notes/prediction_map.md) and [`SPACE_AND_GRAVITY.md`](../SPACE_AND_GRAVITY.md) (Classical source).*

**The question.** Two masses are each put in a superposition of two places and left to interact through gravity only. Do they come out entangled?

**Two answers.**
- *Gravity as a quantum interaction* (the Newtonian potential acting as an operator, or any quantum mediator): yes. Each pair of branches gathers its own phase, φ = G m₁m₂ τ/(ħ r) for a pair a distance r apart, and the differences between pairs entangle the masses (Bose et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 119, 240401 (2017); Marletto and Vedral, Phys. Rev. Lett. 119, 240402 (2017)). For masses near 10⁻¹⁴ kg, each split by about 250 μm, centres about 450 μm apart, held for about 2.5 s, the phase differences are of order one.
- *The Commitment Ontology* (a proposed framework in which time is a count of irreversible events): no, at any mass, separation or time. Gravity's field is classical, and its source follows what a body has irreversibly interacted with, not the body's undecided state. A classical field acting locally can shift each mass's own phase; it cannot entangle the two.

**What a run must do.**
1. Witness entanglement itself (spin or path correlations that no separable state gives). A phase shift of one mass caused by the other is not the test: a classical field gives that too.
2. Keep every other coupling out. In the 2017 scheme the closest approach, about 200 μm, is set by keeping the Casimir–Polder force below gravity; charges and magnetic moments need the same care. The entangling phase should scale as m₁m₂/r.
3. For a null result to count, show the apparatus could have seen it: each mass alone keeps its coherence for the full time, the measured loss of coherence is slower than the expected entangling rate, and the same witness does detect entanglement from a known coupling of similar size (the masses brought closer, or a small applied charge).

**What a result would mean.** Entanglement through gravity alone, at the rate and with the scaling of the first answer, would show CO's account of gravity to be wrong. (Whether a classical field could still give a much smaller entanglement through matter's own quantum processes is disputed: Aziz and Howl, Nature 646, 813 (2025); Marletto, Oppenheim, Vedral and Wilson, arXiv:2511.07348.) No entanglement, with the three checks of item 3 passed, would count against gravity as a quantum interaction and for a classical field; it would not pick CO out from other classical-gravity models.

**Where CO differs from other classical-gravity models.** CO adds no collapse of its own and no noise made by gravity: a body's field is updated only when the body interacts irreversibly with something else (light, gas, a detector). So it expects no heating or noise made by gravity itself. The noise it does carry is set by how often the body interacts; how the decoherence–diffusion trade-off of classical gravity (Oppenheim et al., Nature Commun. 14, 7910 (2023)) applies to a well-isolated body is not yet worked out. It also expects no line from self-gravity in a continuously measured oscillator (as worked out for the causal-conditional case by Liu et al., arXiv:2207.05966). A number that separates CO from mean-field gravity for one isolated superposed mass is not yet worked out.

**Where things stand.** The largest mass put in a spatial superposition is seven to eight orders of magnitude below what the 2017 scheme needs; gravity between masses of about 90 mg has been measured (Westphal et al., Nature 591, 225 (2021)), classically. Schemes that shorten the route keep the mass gap. The test is decades away at the present pace.
